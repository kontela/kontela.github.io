Method: a scripted triage pass ranked every genre's texts by transliteration completeness (free, no LLM cost), keeping the top 300 per genre (or the full set, for genres smaller than that). Ten parallel agents then each read one genre's curated set directly off the source data and wrote up what stood out — translating untranslated Akkadian/Sumerian themselves where needed, with confidence flagged per item. Where a tablet already carries a published scholarly translation (a #tr.en line in the source), that's marked high confidence; everything else is a working render, not a citation-grade edition — treat accordingly.

I · The Genre Blind Spot

CDLI's own genre field is simply blank for entire famous archives — meaning anyone filtering the catalogue by genre never sees them at all. This is the single most useful finding of the sweep: not a hidden tablet, but a hole in how the whole corpus gets searched.

Untagged: the Amarna letters, the Kanesh merchants, the medical canon

Structural findingUnclassified bucket

Inside just the top-300 "Unclassified" (blank-genre) records: 85 are Amarna letters (the 14th-century BC Egypt/Near-East diplomatic archive), 123 are Old Assyrian Kanesh merchant-trade tablets, 44 are BAM medical texts, and 34 are Neo-Babylonian Larsa loan receipts. Four entire, independently famous and well-studied archives — sitting with no genre tag at all.

Why it matters: a genre-filtered search of CDLI for "Letter" or "Scientific" silently misses these. It's a classification gap, not noise.
Source: 300-record Unclassified triage sample · cdli.earth genre search

A governor's unanswered pleas — the Rib-Hadda letters

LetterByblosAmarna · 14th c. BC

At least 21 separate letters in the curated sample alone are from Rib-Hadda, vassal governor of Byblos, repeatedly prostrating himself "7 times and 7 times" to Pharaoh while begging for military aid against the advancing Amurru/Habiru threat.

Why it matters: read in chronological order, these trace one real person's escalating desperation as his city's situation worsens — a known dramatic arc in Amarna scholarship, invisible to anyone genre-filtering CDLI for "Letter."
e.g. EA 137 · P271021 → · Translation confidence: low (formulaic openings clear; substance needs published EA editions)

The opening letter of the Amarna archive

LetterAkhetaten14th c. BC

EA 001 — Pharaoh Amenhotep III writing to Kadašman-Enlil I of Babylon. The foundational document of one of the most-cited diplomatic archives in ancient history, carrying no genre tag at all.

P270887 → · Translation confidence: medium

"What shall I do for myself about this?" — a clay foot's lament

AnomalyUmmaED IIIa · ~2500 BC

An inscribed clay model foot, carrying a first-person plaintive line: a-na ma-szi-ak — "What shall I do for myself about this?" An unusual object type paired with an unusually emotional first-person register for this early a period.

Why it matters: flagged here as a genuine anomaly worth a specialist's look, not a confirmed significant find — exactly the kind of object easy to miss in a "uncertain"-genre bucket of mostly fragmentary economic texts.

II · Ledgers of the Dead

"Administrative" is the largest genre by far (95,794 texts) and the one historians skip fastest — routine ration and labor bookkeeping. Buried in it: a coherent cluster of death-rosters tying captive-laborer mortality to named, datable Ur III military campaigns.

"Barley rations of the captives" — a death-roster of 40 names

AdministrativeUmmaUr III · ~2100–2000 BC

Forty-plus lines, each reading usz2 [Name] — "dead: [Name]." Most names are foreign-sounding, not local Sumerian (hu-ru-ut, ar-bi2-al, tab-ba-u3). The closing line ties the whole list explicitly to sze-ba nam-ra-ak — "barley rations of the captives."

P131753 → · TCL 5, 6039 · Translation confidence: medium

Captive women and their children, dying together

AdministrativeGirsuUr III

Another death-roster, headed geme2 nam-ra-ak-me — "they are captive female workers." Roughly 30 entries, several recording dumu-ni ("her child") dying alongside the mother.

P135732 → · TUT 159 · Translation confidence: medium

Death tolls tied to named wars

AdministrativePuzriš-DaganUr III

Repeated ba-usz2 ("died") entries interleaved with nam-ra kur mar-tu ("booty/captives from the Amorite land") and nam-ra-ak szimaszgi ("captives from Šimaški") — directly linking the deaths to specific, datable Ur III campaigns against the Amorites and Šimaški.

Why it matters: taken together, this cluster chains a real narrative — campaign, captives marched to Umma/Girsu/Drehem, assigned to forced labor gangs, dead within months — out of tablets filed as routine bookkeeping.
P126167 → · PDT 2, 0802 · Translation confidence: medium

The ox that "fled" — a herder's sworn alibi

AdministrativeUmmaUr III

A three-year-old ox died at the threshing floor under a herder named Akišare. The account records his sworn oath that it had instead escaped — nam-erim2-bi ba-da-zah3 su-su a-ki-szar, "its oath: it fled, Akišare must repay."

Why it matters: a real glimpse of oath-based liability procedure for a single missing animal, embedded inside an otherwise dry account.
P208768 → · BM 105329 · Translation confidence: high

III · The Birth of Classification

"Lexical" texts — ancient word lists — are usually dismissed as dry reference material. They're actually the earliest evidence of humans systematically categorizing the world, centuries before literature or law appear in the same corpus.

The oldest dictionary bridging two unrelated languages

LexicalEbla~2350–2250 BC

A bilingual glossary pairing Sumerian logograms with Eblaite (early West Semitic) pronunciations. Sumerian is a language isolate; Eblaite is Semitic, like Akkadian and Hebrew. Scribes in Ebla — in modern Syria, far from Sumer — built this cross-language reference tool roughly 700 years before the famous Sumerian-Akkadian bilinguals usually cited as the earliest of their kind.

P218313 → · Translation confidence: high on the structural claim

A curriculum that traveled 700km and 500 years

LexicalŠuruppak & Ebla2900–2250 BC

The "ED Officials" word list appears nearly verbatim at two sites half a millennium and 700km apart — opening with the same sequence (ukkin "assembly," gal5-la2 "bailiff," lagar, kisal, en-nun "watch"...).

Why it matters: direct material evidence of a standardized scribal curriculum carried from southern Mesopotamia to Syria — children in Ebla drilled on the same official-titles list as children centuries earlier in Sumer.
P499153 → vs. P218312 → · Translation confidence: high on the match

The oldest gazetteer in human history

LexicalED period~2900–2700 BC

A scribal list of city names — Ur, Nippur, Larsa, Uruk, Adab, Kesh, Zabalam, Eresh — from roughly 4,700 years ago. A trainee-scribe "top cities of Sumer" list; whether the ordering reflects size, religious importance, or travel route is an open question the underlying data could support investigating.

P499077 → · Translation confidence: high on city identifications

Insurance-grade sheep accounting, 4,000 years ago

LexicalNippur / KisurraOld Babylonian

The Ur5-ra encyclopedic word list devotes dozens of entries to sheep, categorized by exact cause of loss: "sheep eaten by a god," "sheep eaten by a lion," "sheep separated from the herd," "fallen sheep." Lost livestock gets coded by cause exactly the way a modern insurance claim would be.

IV · Medicine and Magic

Almost the entire Scientific/medical genre (298 of 300 curated texts) has never been officially translated into English — this is the genre where original deep-linked reading adds the most real value, since most of it isn't accessible anywhere else online.

An epilepsy cure, half pharmacology, half spell

ScientificAssurNeo-Assyrian

A head-ailment compendium treats antašubbû ("the falling sickness," epilepsy, attributed to demonic attack) with a seven-plant mixture drunk in beer or wine "while reciting the incantation 'Ea has cast it down.'"

Why it matters: one recipe shows the dual-track Mesopotamian medical logic in a single act — real multi-plant pharmacology bound to a ritual invocation of the god of wisdom and magic.
P285254 → · BAM 2, 159 · Translation confidence: medium

A household first-aid manual — toothache treated on the wrong side, on purpose

ScientificOld Babylonian

A sequence of ~20 prescriptions: jaundice cured with liquorice root left "under the stars" overnight; a dog bite treated explicitly "to prevent the puppies from being engendered" inside the victim (a folk rabies theory); and a toothache remedy poured onto the side of the face opposite the pain — sympathetic logic, not anatomy.

P285464 → · BAM 4, 393 · Translation confidence: high (official translation)

"Hand of a Ghost" — a named diagnosis for chronic illness

ScientificAssurNeo-Assyrian

A patient with a swollen belly who talks deliriously "like a screaming wagon" is diagnosed: the illness has become chronic — it is ŠU-GIDIM-MA, "Hand of a Ghost" — a formally recognized diagnostic category attributing chronic disease to a ghost's affliction, treated with an elaborate aromatic potion.

P369126 → · BAM 2, 168 / KAR 157 · Translation confidence: medium

The last gasp of cuneiform medicine, 400 BC

ScientificBabylonAchaemenid

The latest-dated text in the entire sweep: a counter-witchcraft ritual, 25 plants against "evil sorcery, curse, and oath," followed by an incantation spoken in the voice of "the night-watch, sister of Marduk."

Why it matters: the same witchcraft-affliction framework documented a thousand years earlier was still in active use at the very end of cuneiform's productive life — roughly contemporary with classical Greece.
P480845 → · JMC 26, 001-032 · Translation confidence: low-medium

V · Letters Across Four Millennia

The Letter genre skews toward two well-studied archives (Mari royal correspondence, Kanesh merchant trade), so the value here isn't novelty — it's that over a third of this curated set already carries publication-quality translation.

A goddess, speaking through a woman in trance, threatens the king

LetterMariOld Babylonian

"Zimri-Lim, if you disregard me, I myself over you am bursting (with love); your enemies..."

Queen Šibtu reports to King Zimri-Lim that a woman named Aḫātum went into ecstatic trance in the temple of Annunītum and spoke as the goddess herself.

Why it matters: direct evidence of how Mari's court used religious ecstatics as a political-intelligence and legitimacy channel.
P349972 → · ARM 10, 008 · Translation confidence: high

"My garments only get worse, yours improve"

LetterLarsaOld Babylonian

Iddin-Sin writes to Zinu: "The garments of others year for year are improving, but as for you, my garments year for year you reduce!" A blunt, petty domestic complaint about textile favoritism — could pass for a modern family text message.

P387407 → · TCL 18, 111 · Translation confidence: high

"If you are my brother, by his side stand! Please!"

LetterLarsaOld Babylonian

Batūlum writes to Lu-gisa, begging him to protect her sister's son: "By his side, stand! ... Please!" — short, urgent, and emotionally raw.

The oldest battle report in the set

LetterGirsuED IIIb · ~2500–2340 BC

Lu-ena reports to his superior Enentarzi: 600 Elamites' property carried off from Lagash, a battle fought, "in Elam defeat was placed," 540 Elamites further mentioned. A field report roughly 4,500 years old.

P247594 → · RA 006, 139 · Translation confidence: high

The gourmet who shared his bandicoots

LetterLarsaOld Babylonian

"Just one for my own repast I kept back, and it tasted excellent! How good they were had I but k[nown]..."

Šamaš-nāṣir tells Nūr-Šamaš that of seven bandicoots received as a gift, he sent six onward and kept one for himself — and regretted not keeping more. A rare, charming, food-obsessed personal voice in the corpus.

VI · Court and Contract

A recurring pattern across the strongest legal finds: nadītu priestesses appear as active legal and economic agents — buyers, landlords, manumitters — not bystanders.

Fragments of one of the oldest known international treaties

LegalEbla~2350–2250 BC

The Ebla–Abarsal treaty: a territorial-control register listing border towns "in the hand of the lord of Ebla," and a fugitive-extradition clause naming individuals handed over between the two polities.

P241971 → & P242116 → · ARET 13 · Translation confidence: low-medium

"She has broken the jar of her slavery"

LegalNippurOld Babylonian

"Ištar-utar, a female slave — Nuṭuptum, daughter of Ṣilli-šamaš, has set her free, and her forehead has cleansed; she has broken the jar of her slavery."

A manumission document, witnessed and sealed by Nuṭuptum — herself a nadītu priestess — freeing another woman.

P261082 → · PBS 08/2, 137 · Translation confidence: high

A divorce contract with equal penalties for husband and wife

LegalNippurOld Babylonian

Whichever spouse repudiates the other forfeits the same bridewealth and pays the same additional penalty — symmetric terms, against the common assumption that Old Babylonian divorce law was uniformly harsh on wives. Witnessed by two women.

P262072 → · BE 06/2, 040 · Translation confidence: high

A nadītu priestess as buyer and landlord

LegalNippur / SipparOld Babylonian

In one contract, Lamassum (nadītu of Ninurta) buys a fractional inheritance share of a house for 14 shekels; in another, Ribbatum (nadītu of Šamaš) rents her own house out for a year. Independent female economic agency, in writing.

VII · Kings, Gods, and Cracks in the Propaganda

These are the canonical texts of Assyriology, not unknown material — but real human and procedural detail survives even inside formal royal monuments.

The founding of the first empire

Royal/MonumentalOld Akkadian

Sargon of Akkad defeats Lugalzagesi of Uruk, parades him in a neck-stock to the gate of the god Enlil at Nippur, and claims he "washed his weapons in the sea" — symbolic dominion from the Persian Gulf. The founding text of the first multi-city Mesopotamian empire.

P227509 → · Translation confidence: medium

A king, anxious about a dream, sails to see a specialist

Royal/MonumentalGirsuLagash II

"My dream-interpreter, knowing on her own, may she make its meaning visible for me."

Gudea Cylinder A: the god Ningirsu appears to Gudea in a dream commanding him to build a temple. Gudea wakes unable to understand it, and sails by barge to the goddess Nanše's temple specifically for a professional interpretation.

Why it matters: unusually intimate, emotionally narrated content for a royal building inscription.
P431881 → · Translation confidence: high

A king's land deed, carved as a monument

Royal/MonumentalSusaOld Akkadian

The Manishtushu Obelisk isn't a glory inscription — it's a giant stone real-estate ledger, naming dozens of land-sellers with exact prices paid in silver and grain. A king turned an ordinary deed into a permanent monument.

P213189 → · OIP 104, 040 · Translation confidence: high on the numerals

Death for a thief — unless her husband is alive to punish her himself

Royal/MonumentalAssurMiddle Assyrian

Middle Assyrian Laws Tablet A: a wife who steals from her sick or dead husband's house is executed along with whoever received the goods. But if the husband is alive and well, he personally punishes his wife instead, and the recipient loses his ears.

Why it matters: far more procedurally specific than Hammurabi's better-known code on comparable gender/property law.

VIII · School Days

The real story in scribal-exercise tablets isn't any single text — it's the patterns across many copies: what got drilled, how often, and how far a fixed curriculum traveled.

A proverb copied at least 16 times

SchoolUrOld Babylonian

"A dog going to a banquet saw a bone and stopped: 'Where I go, more of this — I will eat,' he said. The 'inheritance share' of a dog: bones are selected."

Sixteen separate student exemplars of this exact proverb survive in just the top-300-by-completeness sample — clearly a flagship memorization piece at the Ur scribal school.

P346310 → · CDLI Literary 000799 · Translation confidence: high

A curriculum text that outlived the empire that wrote it

SchoolAssur & SipparNeo-Assyrian → Neo-Babylonian

The same lament/incantation composite text was copied by students in Assur under the Assyrian empire, and again later by students in Sippar after Assyria's fall — physical proof a fixed school text survived the collapse of the state that produced it.

P381794 → et al. · Translation confidence: low-medium

A line on inherited guilt, drilled as handwriting practice

SchoolUrOld Babylonian

"A son who bears no guilt — his mother has not borne him on any day."

A wisdom-literature line asserting universal inherited human guilt, copied by students as a literacy exercise nearly 3,700 years before comparable ideas appear in later religious traditions.

P346414 → · CDLI Literary 000823 · Translation confidence: medium-high

"Princess, daughter of Zali"

SchoolProvenience uncertainOld Babylonian

A student practices a name-and-filiation formula, then practices rendering the same name as a seal impression — training in both scribal and sealing conventions on one tablet.

IX · The Canon, Deep-Linked

Nothing here is unidentified — this is the standard Sumerian and Akkadian literary canon. The story is the traceability: every line of a famous myth pinned to the specific clay tablet, and sometimes the named student, that preserved it.

A Mesopotamian sitcom, and the apprentice who copied it

LiterarySultantepeNeo-Assyrian

"The Poor Man of Nippur": a destitute man tricked and humiliated by a corrupt mayor gets revenge in three escalating disguises. Its colophon names the actual scribe-in-training who copied it: "Nabû-riḫtu-uṣur, apprentice, son of the academy."

Why it matters: we know literally who transcribed a thousand-year-old joke, and where he trained.
P499186 → tale · P338355 → colophon · Translation confidence: high

The earliest evidence of bicameral political deliberation

LiteraryUrukOld Babylonian copy

"Gilgamesh and Aga": before going to war, Gilgamesh consults first an assembly of elders, then an assembly of fighting men — frequently cited as the earliest textual evidence for a two-chamber deliberative body in Mesopotamian political history.

P432478 → · Translation confidence: high

The Babylonian "Job"

LiteraryNeo-Assyrian

"Ludlul bēl nēmeqi" ("I will praise the lord of wisdom") — a man stripped of status, health, and friends laments, then is restored by Marduk. The earliest sustained literary meditation on undeserved suffering in the corpus.

P491222 → · Translation confidence: high

A drinking song — not a hymn, not a contract

LiteraryOld Babylonian

"The gakkul (vat), the gakkul (vat)! ... that puts us in a happy mood!"

A genuinely rare slice-of-life survival — celebratory, lighthearted, and entirely secular against a corpus otherwise dominated by religion, law, and bureaucracy.

X · Stars, Numbers, Spirits

Smaller genres (omen, astronomical, mathematical, ritual, votive) — fewer texts, but among the highest hit-rate of the whole sweep.

Naming the five planets, distinct from the fixed stars

AstronomicalNeo-Babylonian

MUL.APIN Tablet 1 — named in its own colophon — lists ~70 stars and constellations across three celestial "paths," gives a heliacal-rising calendar, and singles out Jupiter, Venus, Mars, Saturn, and Mercury by name as objects that "keep changing position and cross the sky." One of the foundational texts of Babylonian astronomy.

P479385 → · CT 33 · Translation confidence: high

A worked area-calculation table, 600 years before "Old Babylonian math"

MathematicalZabalamED IIIb · ~2500 BC

A square-number land-area table — side lengths squared and converted to area in land-survey units, with the arithmetic spelled out — predating the famous Old Babylonian mathematical corpus by six to seven centuries.

Sargon of Akkad, used as a stock omen centuries after his death

OmenOld Babylonian

A 673-line omen compendium opens with "weapon of Sargon" as a recurring apodosis tied to palace-conspiracy and succession portents. A real historical king, centuries dead, turned into a literary/divinatory archetype.

"May his spirits drink pure water" — a tomb curse and blessing
